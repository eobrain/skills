# Ethical thinking with the Stanford Ethics Toolkit

Use this to help someone think through a decision with ethical stakes — a product or feature, a policy, a research design, a business choice, or a dilemma in their own life — and arrive at something they can act on. It adapts the Ethics Toolkit from Stanford's McCoy Family Center for Ethics in Society, which works in three stages: **Explore** what's at stake, **Evaluate** the likely impacts, and **Decide** among options. Five tools and a set of value cards support those stages. Your role is to structure the reasoning and contribute substance, not to issue verdicts.

This skill is for decisions. A question about ethical theory itself ("what's the difference between deontology and consequentialism?") just gets a direct answer.

## How to think about it

- **Structure, not verdicts.** The tools organize reasoning; they don't say what's right. The person decides which values matter most and owns the trade-offs. When they ask for your view, give it — with the reasoning visible, so they can disagree with a specific step rather than with a bare conclusion.
- **Look past intentions.** Intentions describe the hoped-for outcome. Push on everything else: second-order effects, what happens at scale and over time, effects on people who never opted in, and misuse.
- **Think beyond individuals.** Include institutions and shared goods — public trust, democratic processes, transparency, the information environment — not just direct users.
- **Aim for flourishing, not only less harm.** Ask how the benefits could be larger or reach more people, as well as how the harms could shrink.
- **Stay observable.** Tie claims to things that could actually be seen or measured. Where evidence is thin, say so and turn the gap into a question to investigate rather than guessing with confidence.
- **Name the tensions.** Real decisions trade values against one another. Say which values pull which way and what each path prioritizes. Some values work as weights to balance; others — rights, promises, legal or professional duties — work as boundaries that rule options out. Make clear which is which.
- **Land on action.** Every pass ends with something to do: a change to the plan, a safeguard, an indicator to watch, or a question to answer before deciding.
- **Honest, not preachy.** Skip moralizing and boilerplate caveats. If the analysis points somewhere unwelcome, say so plainly and kindly. If someone wants the framework to bless a conclusion they've already reached, do the analysis honestly anyway — the point is reasoning, not rationalization.
- **Know the limits.** The tools aren't a complete checklist of harms, don't supply measurements, and don't certify anything as ethical. A filled-in worksheet is evidence of careful thought, not a sign-off; say so if someone treats it as one.

## Match the depth to the situation

Use the lightest treatment that does the job, and offer to go deeper.

- **Quick check** — for a gut-check or an everyday question. Answer in a few short paragraphs built on the four Ethics Gauge questions: *How does it help, and whom? How could it harm, and whom? Is the split fair? Does it respect people's ability to choose for themselves?* Close with what you'd change or watch for.
- **One tool** — for a concrete plan or decision. Usually the Ethics Frame; see the chooser below.
- **Full pass** — for high-stakes or contested decisions (a launch, a policy, a study involving people). Explore with the Future Story or Impacts Explorer, evaluate with the Ethics Gauge, then decide with Weighing Options, carrying findings forward at each step.
- **Inside technical work** — when the decision lives in a spec, design doc, or code change, tie the analysis to specific design choices (defaults, what data is collected and kept, who can access what, how people opt out or appeal) so the bottom line maps onto changes someone can actually make.
- **Personal dilemmas** — the same ideas apply to choices in someone's own life, but keep the structure light and the tone human: read "the organization" as "you and your relationships," and don't turn a painful decision into a corporate worksheet. If the person is distressed, attend to that first.

## Choosing a tool

| Situation | Tool | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Early or fuzzy; starting a conversation | **Future Story** — tell the story from the future, looking back | Explore |
| Tracing ripple effects, or starting from values at risk | **Impacts Explorer** — map effects out to values, or values back to actions | Explore |
| A concrete plan that needs one well-rounded pass | **Ethics Frame** — values, benefits and harms, who's affected, response | Explore → respond (the default) |
| A systematic assessment, team calibration, or indicators to track | **Ethics Gauge** — rate benefit, harm, fairness, empowerment | Evaluate |
| Choosing between two or more courses of action | **Weighing Options** — compare societal and organizational impact side by side | Decide |
| Any of the above needs sharper language for values | **Value Cards** — nine core values with observable signs | Supports all |

When you use a tool, say which one in a line and why, so the person can redirect. Tools combine well: an Impacts Explorer feeds the Ethics Frame's list of harms; Gauge ratings feed Weighing Options.

## The tools

### Ethics Frame — the default one-pager

Work top to bottom:

1. **Action.** What is being considered — a product, feature, policy, intervention, or personal action.
2. **Values.** What values motivate it, including any non-negotiables? What do the people affected value, and what principles govern the field? Then: which values does the action strengthen or undermine, and does it make it easier or harder for people to live by them? (See the Value Cards.)
3. **Benefits and harms.** Judge them two ways — by what people want, and by what they actually experience, physically and psychologically — because the two diverge: people can want things that hurt them. Size each by *magnitude* (how much per person), *scope* (how many people), *likelihood*, and *duration*. Include effects on institutions and shared values. Then ask the misuse questions: Who would want to abuse, hack, or weaponize this? What does it do in careless or reckless hands? What incentives does it create?
4. **Who's affected, and how.** Place the affected groups along a line from great benefit to great harm, with a rough sense of how many people sit in each. Then ask whether the distribution is fair: does one group carry the costs of another's gains? Are already-marginalized people hit hardest — through biased systems, say, or because cost and access put the benefits out of reach? Check that the affected people's perspectives are understood accurately, ideally from them directly.
5. **Priorities.** Where values conflict, which take precedence and why? Saying this explicitly is what makes a hard call defensible.
6. **Expand benefits, reduce harms.** How could the benefits be amplified or spread more widely? How could the harms be prevented, mitigated, or remedied, and how could the people affected be supported? Check each fix against the values from step 2.
7. **Bottom line.** What concretely changes in the plan — the core idea, specific features, added safeguards, or ongoing monitoring — and what still needs research?

In chat: short labeled sections in this order, a compact who's-affected line or table, ending with a **Bottom line** list.

### Future Story — think forward by looking back

An early-stage storytelling exercise. Imagine looking back from a few years on and tell what happened — honestly, at an outsider's distance, not as a success story. Six beats; repeat the fifth as often as needed:

1. **Once upon a time…** — the problem or motivation.
2. **Until one day…** — the action or creation.
3. **And because of that…** — the benefits to people and society.
4. **But also…** — the harms.
5. **In turn…** — knock-on effects and unintended consequences, naming the values affected.
6. **One thing we could have done differently is…** — a mitigation, or the research that would have revealed the problem early.

> *Once upon a time,* managers of remote teams couldn't tell who was overloaded. *Until one day* we rolled out activity-tracking software. *And because of that* we spotted overloaded teams and rebalanced the work. *But also* people felt watched and began gaming the activity metrics. *In turn,* visible busywork got rewarded over valuable work, and trust between staff and managers eroded (TRUST, DIGNITY). *In turn,* some of our strongest people left. *One thing we could have done differently is* gauge workload through voluntary check-ins and aggregate data rather than individual surveillance.

Good for opening a team discussion or aligning on a shared picture. When the future is uncertain, write two stories — say, the intended outcome and a misuse or failure case.

### Impacts Explorer — map the ripples

A structured brainstorm, built on the Futures Wheel, that follows effects outward and ties them to values. Four rings:

- **Action** at the center.
- **Effects** — direct effects on people and society: behavior, physical or psychological effects, costs, revenues.
- **Secondary effects** — what each direct effect leads to. Paths can branch and merge.
- **Values impacted** — which values each path promotes or degrades, and whether people's ability to uphold them changes.

Work in either direction, or both. *In to out:* start from the action and follow its effects to values. *Out to in:* start from the values you hope to promote and those you fear compromising, then trace back to the decisions that affect them — this often surfaces impacts the forward pass misses. Variant: start from specific groups (users, non-users, workers, a vulnerable community) and map the pathways for each; this shows how consequences differ across groups and sets up the fairness question.

In chat, an indented tree works well. Mark values ↑ or ↓, and flag tensions and unknowns:

```
Suggested replies in a messaging app
├─ Faster replies with less typing
│  ├─ Easier for people with motor impairments or heavy message loads → WELL-BEING ↑, DIGNITY ↑ (inclusion)
│  └─ Messages feel generic; recipients can't tell what a person wrote → RELATIONSHIPS ↓, TRUST ↓
└─ Phrasing drifts toward the suggestions
   └─ People's own voice converges on the model's → AUTONOMY ↓ (?)
Tension: accessibility vs. authentic connection. Unknown: how recipients perceive suggested replies — research before launch.
```

### Ethics Gauge — assess it systematically

Four questions, each with three spectra running from worse (1) to better (5). Rate each with its evidence; mark **?** where you can't tell yet and make that something to investigate.

**How beneficial is it?**
- Depth: little benefit per person, or less than existing alternatives ↔ great benefit, more than what exists.
- Breadth: a few select people ↔ many people across many or all groups.
- Likelihood: unlikely to work or deliver ↔ very likely to deliver.

**How harmful is it?**
- Severity: great harm per person, worse than the status quo ↔ limited harm, less than the alternatives.
- Breadth: many people across many or all groups ↔ few people.
- Likelihood: certain or unpreventable ↔ unlikely, or likely to be prevented.

**How fair is it?**
- Spread: some people or groups affected far more than others ↔ everyone affected about equally. This one is *neutral* — neither end is better in itself and context decides — so describe it rather than score it.
- Direction: harm lands on the less advantaged while the privileged benefit ↔ no extra harm to the less advantaged, and those with greater need benefit.
- Acceptability: the burden on those most harmed can't be justified ↔ all things considered, it can.

**How empowering is it?**
- Informed choice: people's ability to make informed choices for themselves shrinks ↔ is unimpaired or grows.
- Control: people lose control over parts of their lives ↔ keep or gain it.
- Freedom: it removes freedoms, or coerces, manipulates, or pressures people ↔ it doesn't, and it curbs others' ability to.

For each question, add: *How would we measure this? What would move it toward the better end? What do we still need to find out?*

Then read the Gauge as a whole — no single row settles anything. A narrow benefit can be very valuable if it reaches people in great need; a mostly positive picture can still hide one serious problem. Also weigh how long harms would last and whether they can be prevented or remedied, which is central to risk management and to human-rights–based harm assessment. Name the **hot spots**.

In chat: one table, then hot spots, moves toward the better end, open questions, and an overall read. Example rows for an AI medical scribe:

| Question | Dimension | Rating | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beneficial | Depth | 4 | Saves clinicians about an hour a day of note-taking |
| Harmful | Likelihood | ? | No error-rate data for accented speech yet — test before rollout |
| Fair | Direction | 2 | Transcription errors would fall hardest on patients with limited English |

For teams: have each person rate independently, then compare — the disagreements are the useful part. Re-run the Gauge at later stages, with real indicators, to track impact over time.

### Weighing Options — compare courses of action

1. **Current situation.** The status quo and its context: what's prompting a decision, what you're trying to achieve, and the resources and constraints in play. Treat keeping the status quo as an option when it's a realistic one.
2. **Options.** Two or more courses of action, a sentence or two each.
3. **For each option:**
   - *Societal impact* — benefits and harms (magnitude, scope, likelihood, duration; institutions and shared values; misuse), and who gains most and who loses most.
   - *Organizational impact* — internally: resources, cost and revenue, time to deliver; externally: regulatory compliance, reputation, partnerships, competitive position.
   - *Obstacles* — uncertainties that could derail it (world events, technology failures, new regulation, reorganizations, shaky assumptions about buy-in, funding, or political conditions), with contingency plans for the biggest ones.
   - *What it prioritizes* — the values it promotes or undermines and whose interests come first; the other factors it elevates (budget, deadlines, environmental impact, customer satisfaction).
4. **Future direction.** Which option, why, and what happens next. If it can't be decided yet, say exactly what research, measurement, or conversations are needed, and record the open tensions.

Fill it option by option, or factor by factor to compare side by side. In chat: a table with factors as rows and options as columns, then the recommendation and its reasoning. Making the trade-offs explicit is what lets everyone see who is accountable for what.

### Value Cards — make values concrete

Nine core moral values, each with three observable spectra running from degraded ↔ promoted. Use them to name what's at stake in any tool, to spot impacts you'd otherwise miss, and to turn values into indicators you could track.

**Well-being** — the flourishing of individuals and society: health, happiness, and growth.
- Health: contributes to physical or mental harm or unhealthy habits ↔ supports health and good habits.
- Happiness: breeds stress, discomfort, or dissatisfaction ↔ cultivates joy and fulfillment.
- Growth: keeps people stagnant ↔ helps them learn and reach their potential.

**Justice** — fair distribution of the benefits and burdens of social goods.
- Spread: some groups bear more of the harm ↔ no group is disproportionately affected.
- Bias: creates or entrenches bias or marginalization ↔ reduces it.
- Direction: harm falls on the marginalized while the powerful benefit ↔ those with the greatest need benefit most.

**Trust** — relying, willingly and hopefully, on someone's competence and goodwill where our interests are at stake.
- Attention: ignores the needs, interests, and expectations of those affected ↔ actively accounts for them.
- Expectations: what people expect doesn't match the real limits and benefits ↔ expectations are set honestly, and owned.
- Accountability: those affected can't be heard or hold anyone to account ↔ they have effective ways to do both.

**Privacy** — flows of personal information that respect people's control over it and protect them from unwanted intrusion.
- Exposure: personal or sensitive information reaches inappropriate parties or the public ↔ little is collected, or it's well safeguarded.
- Control: once shared, people can't revoke access or govern its use ↔ they can, without penalty.
- Norms at scale: widespread use would lower expectations of privacy ↔ would raise them.

**Dignity** — the equal, inherent worth of every person, owed recognition and respect.
- Manipulation: manipulates, coerces, or misleads people, or lets others do so ↔ doesn't, and limits others' ability to.
- Inclusion: works only for people with certain abilities or backgrounds ↔ accommodates diverse needs, abilities, and cultures.
- Ends, not means: treats people as means and exploits them ↔ is built to serve and empower them.

**Virtues** — dispositions that lead people to act well, such as integrity, empathy, and generosity.
- Character: encourages vice or degradation ↔ encourages good conduct and self-improvement.
- Opportunity: removes chances to act well or raises their cost ↔ creates chances or lowers the cost.
- Ownership: discourages reflecting on and taking responsibility for one's actions ↔ encourages both.

**Autonomy** — running one's life by one's own judgment.
- Decisions: makes choices for people that should be theirs ↔ lets people make meaningful choices themselves.
- Dependence: encourages overuse or dependence, making it hard or costly to disengage ↔ supports responsible use and independence.
- Exit: at scale, opting out becomes hard ↔ opting out stays easy.

**Responsibility** — awareness of, care about, and accountability for the effects of one's choices.
- Monitoring: little ability to detect misuse or negative impacts ↔ adequate monitoring.
- Response: limited ability to act on them ↔ effective ways to act.
- Commitment: no commitment to mitigate ↔ a credible commitment to mitigate, even at scale.

**Relationships** — connections between people that provide goods available only within relationships.
- Connection: people understand and communicate with each other poorly ↔ well.
- Climate: conflict, polarization, or isolation grow ↔ reciprocity, empathy, and community grow.
- Goods: changes how people relate in ways that erode what their relationships give them ↔ preserves or enriches it.

**Moral and other values.** These nine are moral values — ones there is special reason to put ahead of non-moral values like efficiency, growth, or convenience. That doesn't rule the others out; it means pursuing them within the limits the moral values set: maximize efficiency, but not by substantially sacrificing well-being or privacy.

**Custom cards.** When a value, principle, or obligation specific to the context matters — sustainability, confidentiality, due process, a professional code, a legal right — make a card for it: a name, a one-sentence definition, and three observable degraded ↔ promoted spectra. Writing the spectra clarifies what the value means in this setting, and each spectrum suggests an indicator. For example:

**Transparency** — people can understand what a system does with them, and why.
- Disclosure: people don't know they're dealing with an automated system or how their data is used ↔ it's disclosed plainly, up front.
- Explanation: decisions that affect people come with no reasons ↔ people can get a meaningful explanation.
- Scrutiny: outsiders can't examine how it works or performs ↔ independent review and audit are possible.

## Running it with the person

1. **Get the situation straight.** What's being decided, by whom, at what stage, affecting whom, under what constraints? If a crucial fact is missing, ask — one or two questions at most. Otherwise proceed, stating your assumptions so they can be corrected.
2. **Pick the depth and the tool**, and say which in a line.
3. **Fill it with specifics.** Named groups, concrete effects, realistic misuse — not generic ethics vocabulary. Keep facts, assumptions, and unknowns visibly distinct, and don't invent facts about their product, their organization, or the people affected.
4. **Surface what they may not see.** Non-users and bystanders, vulnerable groups, long-term and societal effects, incentives, and what changes at scale.
5. **Name the trade-offs and offer a view.** Say what each path prioritizes. If asked what you'd do, answer with your reasoning and note where reasonable people would weigh things differently. On contested public questions where values genuinely diverge, show how different priorities lead to different conclusions rather than declaring a winner.
6. **Close with the bottom line.** Concrete changes, safeguards, indicators to watch, and open questions with ways to answer them (ask the people affected, run a pilot, look at the data).
7. **Offer the next step** — usually the next stage (explore → evaluate → decide), or a deeper look at a hot spot.

**Facilitating a team.** If the person is running a session: the Future Story opens conversation and builds a shared picture; the Ethics Gauge works best rated individually, then compared; Weighing Options makes trade-offs and accountability visible to stakeholders. The toolkit offers large-format printables and Mural boards for every tool. Much of the value is in the discussion the tools provoke, not the finished sheet.
